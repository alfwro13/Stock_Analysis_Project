🇺🇸 US Market Context

{{ sentiment_html | safe }}
How to read this chart:
This visualization tracks the stock market's "Health" (S&P 500) against Wall Street's "Emotion" (Fear & Greed Index). When the red dotted line crashes below 25, the market is panicking—which is historically the safest time to buy. When it spikes above 75, investors are overly greedy, meaning a sell-off is highly likely.
{{ vix_spy_html | safe }}
How to read this chart:
The VIX is Wall Street's "Fear Gauge". It measures how much money investors are spending on crash insurance. If the VIX stays below 20, the market is calm and safe. If it spikes above 20, conditions are choppy and unpredictable. A spike above 30 means pure panic and high risk.
{{ yield_equity_html | safe }}
US Cost of Capital Explained:
Think of the 30-Year Treasury Yield as the ultimate "interest rate" for the economy. When this red line spikes, borrowing money becomes expensive for companies and consumers, which suffocates economic growth and crushes stock prices. Conversely, falling yields act as rocket fuel for bull markets.
{{ yield_curve_html | safe }}
Yield Curve Radar (T10Y2Y) Explained:
Normally, locking your money up in a 10-Year bond pays more interest than a 2-Year bond. However, when investors panic about a near-term economic crash, they rush to the 10-Year safety, driving its payout below the 2-Year. When this line drops below zero into the red zone, it is Wall Street's ultimate recession warning.
{{ us_liquidity_html | safe }}
US Liquidity (M2) Explained:
M2 Money Supply represents the raw "fuel" in the economy—literally the amount of cash sitting in bank accounts and circulation. Expanding liquidity makes it incredibly easy for the stock market to rise. If the stock market is rising while this yellow line is falling, the rally is running on empty fumes.
{{ us_credit_html | safe }}
US High Yield Spread Explained:
"ICE BofA" is just the data provider. The "High Yield Spread" simply measures the penalty interest rate that risky companies have to pay to get loans. If this red line spikes into the Danger Zone (>5.0%), banks are terrified of bankruptcies and are refusing to lend. The stock market rarely survives when credit markets panic.

🇬🇧 UK Market Context

{{ ftse_gbp_html | safe }}
How to read this chart:
Because the biggest UK companies make most of their money overseas in Dollars and Euros, a weak Pound (GBP) is actually great for the FTSE 100. Their foreign profits look much bigger when converted back to a weak home currency. Look for the blue and green lines to move in opposite directions.
{{ uk_yield_equity_html | safe }}
UK Cost of Capital Explained:
Just like the US, rising borrowing costs (the 10-Year Gilt Yield) act as gravity on the UK stock market. High yields mean expensive mortgages for consumers and expensive loans for businesses, which drags down the FTSE 100.
{{ uk_liquidity_html | safe }}
UK Liquidity (M4) Explained:
M4 represents the total cash flowing through the UK economy. A growing money supply acts as a safety net, supporting business expansion and stock market rallies. A contracting money supply pulls that safety net away, making it very hard for the market to grow.
{{ uk_credit_html | safe }}
UK IG Spread Explained:
This measures the financial stress of high-quality UK corporations. If this line crosses into the Danger Zone (>3.0%), it means investors are demanding massive premiums to lend money to even the safest UK companies. This signals a severe economic freeze.
