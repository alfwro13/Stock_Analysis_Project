What the Strategy Backtester Does

In plain terms: it replays the past. You pick a basket of tickers and a few ways of splitting the money between them, and it shows what each way would have turned the starting capital into, day by day, so you can compare the rules against each other before trusting any of them.

More precisely, the Strategy Backtester (Tools menu) simulates rules such as Buy-and-Hold, Rebalanced Equal Weight, Current Portfolio Weights, Inverse-Volatility Weighting, Tolerance-Band Rebalancing and rolling versions of the Portfolio Optimizer's Steadiest and Best Reward-for-Risk mixes over the daily prices this app already stores. It keeps explicit cash, charges trading costs, lets weights drift between rebalances and compares everything with a benchmark. It is a comparison tool only: this app has no order execution, and a past result is not a forecast.

Fixed-Current-Basket Test (Survivorship Bias)

Every backtest here uses the tickers you hold or follow today, replayed over the past. Companies that failed, were dropped from an index or were sold along the way are simply not in the basket, so the results look better than an honest investor in those years could have achieved. That is called survivorship bias.

Basket membership is frozen at the time of the run (the Stable Shortlist basket is frozen at its snapshot date). Results compare rules on the same basket; they do not estimate what an investor would actually have earned.

For that reason the Backtester labels every run a fixed-current-basket test. Use it to ask "which rule behaved better on these tickers?", not "how much would I have made?".

Next-Close Execution

A strategy decides using the closing prices of a day, but you could not have traded at a price you only learned when the market closed. So a decision made after day t's close is carried out at the closing price of the next eligible day, and until then the old holdings keep earning their returns.

Decision uses completed closes with date ≤ t. Execution happens at the first later session on which every ticker in the basket has a real closing price. A day with a known holiday or a missing price is skipped, never traded on.

This removes the most common backtesting cheat: profiting from a move that had already happened by the time the decision was made. The decision and execution times are saved in UTC with every run.

Rebalance Cadence and Weight Drift

Rebalance Cadence is how often a strategy restores its target split: monthly, quarterly or annually, always decided on the last trading day of the period. Between rebalances nothing is traded, so the winners grow into a bigger share and the losers shrink. That is weight drift.

Holdings are kept as quantities between rebalances. Applying fixed target weights to every day's returns would quietly simulate rebalancing every single day, so the Backtester never does that.

Buy-and-Hold never rebalances, so its weights drift for the whole test. Rebalanced Equal Weight and the other cadence strategies pull the weights back at each date, paying trading costs to do so.

Tolerance-Band Rebalancing

A calmer rebalancing habit: start equal weight, then leave everything alone until one holding drifts further than a set number of percentage points from its target. Only then trade back to target. It trades far less than a calendar rule, and only when it matters.

Every session: if max |current weight − target weight| > Tolerance Band (default 5 pp), rebalance to equal weight at the next eligible close.

Unlike the other rebalancing strategies it checks every session, so the Rebalance Cadence setting does not apply to it.

Inverse-Volatility Weighting

A simple way to give calmer tickers more room: each ticker's weight is proportional to 1 divided by its recent volatility, so a steady ticker gets a bigger share than a jumpy one. It needs no forecast of returns, which makes it robust, and its weights always add up to 100%.

wi = (1 / σi) / Σj (1 / σj), where σ is the standard deviation of daily returns over the Training Lookback, restored on every rebalance date. A ticker with zero or undefined volatility makes the decision unavailable instead of producing a made-up weight.
Rolling Strategies and the Training Lookback

The Rolling Steadiest Mix and Rolling Best Reward-for-Risk Mix strategies re-run the Portfolio Optimizer's long-only maths at every rebalance date, but using only the past trading days up to that date, never later prices. The number of past days used is the Training Lookback (default 252, about a year).

Each decision date solves the Long-Only problem (Weight Cap default 20%, Cash Reserve default 0%) on the trailing Training Lookback window. The first decision can only be made once that many sessions exist, so every selected strategy starts on the same day: the latest start any of them needs.

If the solver cannot produce a valid mix on the very first date, or the Weight Cap is too small for the number of tickers, that strategy is reported as unavailable with the reason. Later failed decisions simply keep the existing holdings and are logged.

Trading Costs: Commission, Spread and Slippage

Every buy and sell costs something. Commission is the broker's fee, spread is the gap between the buying and selling price you cross, and slippage is the price moving a little against you before the order fills. The Backtester charges all three as a share of the amount traded, on both buys and sells.

Cost = traded amount × (commission + spread + slippage) in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%). The cost is paid out of the money being invested, so the portfolio after trading is worth what was invested minus the cost. Presets (None, Low 10 bps, Typical 25 bps per side) are editable modelling assumptions, not broker quotes.

Cash is explicit: money is never created to pay costs, and the portfolio can never go negative on cash. UK stamp duty is not modelled.

Net vs Gross Return and Turnover

Net return is what the strategy earned after trading costs. Gross return is what the same rules would have earned with zero costs. The gap between them is the price of the strategy's trading. Turnover says how much trading that was: the share of the portfolio traded in a year.

Turnover per year = Σ (traded amount ÷ 2 ÷ portfolio value at that rebalance) ÷ years tested, one way, not counting the first purchase. The benchmark pays the same costs on its single purchase.

A strategy that beats the others gross but not net is trading too much for its own good.

Extended History

The app normally stores about two years of daily prices, which gives the rolling strategies only about a year to be tested. Extended History downloads about five years of daily prices for just the tickers in your basket (and the benchmark) into a separate cache, so a run can span more than one kind of market.

Prepared through the same price cleaning as the nightly data and saved separately; the nightly refresh and every other page keep using the two-year data. A run uses a ticker's extended history only while it ends within a few days of the standard data, and never mixes the two downloads for one ticker, because prices adjusted at different times do not join cleanly.

The date range each ticker actually covered is always shown with the results.

Incomplete Run

A run is marked Incomplete when a ticker had no closing price on a day its market was open and not on a known holiday. The backtester never invents a price: the previous price is carried only so the portfolio can still be valued, and no trade is ever made using a carried price.

Known exchange closures are carried silently. An unexpected gap is listed with its ticker and date, and every result of the run is flagged because the valuation on that day is uncertain. Fixing the price data (for example with Repair Data) and running again clears it.
