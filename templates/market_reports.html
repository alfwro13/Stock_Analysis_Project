💎 Quality Compounders↑ top

The "Buy and Hold" Screener. High capital efficiency, strong margins, and low debt trading at reasonable valuations.

How to use: These are structurally resilient businesses with proven pricing power and low leverage.
When to use: Use this to build the core foundational pillars of your long-term portfolio.
The Benefit: Filters out both value traps (dying companies) and euphoria traps (overpriced momentum), surfacing only high-quality compounders with a Quant Score >= 60.

Ticker Company Name Sector Close Price ROE Margin Debt/Eq P/E Quant Score
Key Metrics Explained:
  • ROE — Return on Equity (>15%): Think of ROE as a measure of how good the company is at turning shareholder money into profit. If you gave a business £100 to invest and it returned £20 profit, that is an ROE of 20% — excellent. A business returning only £5 on every £100 (5% ROE) is inefficient with your capital. We require >15% to ensure management is genuinely skilled at allocating resources, not just surviving on past glories.
  • Margin — Profit Margin (>10%): For every £1 of revenue the company earns, how many pence does it actually keep as profit after paying all its costs? A 10% margin means it keeps 10p. A 2% margin means it keeps 2p — one bad quarter and that margin disappears entirely. High margins signal a genuine competitive advantage (a "moat"): the company can charge premium prices because customers have few alternatives. Businesses with thin margins are fragile — any cost spike (wages, energy, raw materials) can wipe out profitability overnight.
  • Debt/Equity — Debt to Equity Ratio (<1.0): This tells you how much of the business is funded by borrowed money versus the owners' own money. A ratio of 0.5 means for every £1 of equity, the company has borrowed 50p — conservative and safe. A ratio of 3.0 means the company has borrowed £3 for every £1 it actually owns — dangerously exposed if interest rates rise or revenues dip. We cap at <1.0 because heavily indebted companies can look profitable in good times but collapse when conditions get tough.
  • P/E — Price-to-Earnings Ratio (10–35): The P/E ratio tells you how many years of current earnings you are paying for upfront. A P/E of 20 means you are paying £20 today for every £1 of annual profit the company generates.
    Why does the floor matter (P/E < 10)? Extremely cheap stocks are often cheap for a very good reason — the market expects earnings to collapse. These are called "value traps." You buy thinking it is a bargain, but the business is deteriorating.
    Why does the ceiling matter (P/E > 35)? Very expensive stocks are priced for perfection. Any earnings miss, any slowdown, and the share price falls sharply. These are "euphoria stocks" — great businesses, but terrible entry prices.
    The 10–35 window targets the sweet spot: solid businesses at fair, sustainable valuations.
  • Quant Score (≥60): Our composite score blends all the above metrics — plus momentum and mean-reversion signals — into a single 0–100 number. Think of it as an overall health grade. A score of 60 is a solid B grade: not perfect, but fundamentally sound across every dimension we measure. Stocks below 60 may pass one or two individual screens but show weakness somewhere else in the picture. We require ≥60 to ensure nothing important is hidden.

🚀 GARP — Peter Lynch Tenbaggers↑ top

Growth At a Reasonable Price. Cheap-relative-to-growth equities from the FTSE100 + S&P500 universe that pass Peter Lynch's yield-adjusted PEG threshold.

How to use: Stocks here combine genuine growth (revenue +15%, profitable, growing earnings) with a valuation cheap enough to compound multi-bagger upside.
When to use: Ideal for monthly trading-account top-ups when you want growth exposure without paying euphoria multiples.
The Benefit: Lynch's PEG <= 1.0 ensures you are not overpaying for the growth. The $500M market-cap floor excludes micro-cap pump-and-dumps. The ML confidence column (when populated) adds a quantitative second opinion.

Ticker Company Name Sector Close Price PEG (Lynch) Rev Growth ROE Fwd P/E Market Cap ML
Key Metrics Explained:
  • PEG Ratio — Peter Lynch's "Fair Value" Test (≤1.0): The PEG ratio answers a simple question: are you paying a fair price for the growth you are getting? It is calculated as: Forward P/E ÷ (Earnings Growth % + Dividend Yield %).
    A PEG of 1.0 means the stock's price perfectly reflects its growth rate — you are paying exactly what the growth is worth. A PEG of 0.5 means you are getting £1 of growth for only 50p — a potential bargain. A PEG of 2.0 means you are paying double what the growth is worth — you are funding someone else's optimism.
    Peter Lynch, the legendary Fidelity fund manager who turned $18M into $14 billion, considered PEG ≤1.0 the single most reliable signal of an undervalued growth stock. This screener applies his exact methodology.
  • Revenue Growth (>15% year-on-year): Revenue is the total money flowing into the business before any costs are deducted — it is the raw proof that customers are buying more of what the company sells. A 15% growth rate means the business is 15% bigger than it was a year ago.
    Why does this matter for tenbaggers? A stock that doubles in price typically needs its revenues and earnings to grow substantially to justify that price. Stagnant or slow-growing revenue kills multi-bagger potential. This threshold filters out companies whose earnings grow only because of cost-cutting or accounting adjustments — we want genuine top-line expansion.
  • ROE — Return on Equity (>10%): How efficiently is the business turning your invested capital into profit? (See the Quality Compounders section for a full explanation.)
    This threshold is deliberately looser than the 15% used in Quality Compounders. Fast-growing businesses often reinvest heavily back into themselves — hiring staff, building infrastructure, entering new markets — which temporarily reduces ROE. We accept >10% here to allow genuine growth companies that are spending today to earn more tomorrow.
  • Forward P/E — Price to Future Earnings (10–40): Forward P/E uses next year's expected earnings rather than last year's actual earnings, giving a more forward-looking valuation.
    The floor of 10 removes value traps — companies so cheap the market has given up on their future. The ceiling of 40 is wider than Quality Compounders' 35 because genuine high-growth businesses legitimately command a premium. But even the best growth story is not worth an infinite price — a cap of 40 keeps you anchored to reality.
  • Market Cap — Company Size (>$500M): Market capitalisation is the total value the stock market places on the entire company (share price × total shares outstanding). A $500M floor means we only look at companies worth at least half a billion dollars.
    Why exclude smaller companies? Micro-cap and small-cap stocks can be illiquid — there may not be enough buyers when you want to sell. They are also disproportionately targeted by "pump and dump" schemes and are harder to research reliably. The $500M floor keeps us in the range where institutional investors operate, which provides better price discovery and exit liquidity.
  • ML Confidence — Machine Learning Score (optional): This is a second opinion from our quantitative model, which was trained on historical data to predict whether a stock matching GARP criteria actually outperforms the market over the following 12 months. A score of 75% means the model is 75% confident this stock will outperform based on patterns it has learned.
    Currently most stocks show "—" because ML scoring is still being rolled out across the full universe. Set the Min ML filter to 0 to include all candidates regardless of ML score. As coverage expands, raising this threshold adds a powerful quantitative second opinion on top of Lynch's fundamental screen.

💎 Quality on Sale — 52-Week Low Bargains↑ top

The "Structural Value" Screener. High-quality businesses the market has thrown out with the bathwater — price near a 52-week low, but fundamentals still intact.

How to use: Different from Mean Reversion (which hunts RSI-based panic dips). This report finds companies that have spent months falling — yet remain profitable, low-debt, and high-return on equity.
When to use: Use this to systematically deploy idle cash during corrections. The filter combination separates a falling knife from a genuine bargain.
The Benefit: Long-term investors make their money buying quality businesses when the market is fearful. This report surfaces those moments automatically.

Ticker Company Name Sector Close Price % Above 52w Low ROE Debt/Eq Margin P/E Quant Score
Key Metrics Explained:
  • % Above 52-Week Low: This tells you how close the current price is to the stock's lowest point over the past year. A value of 2% means the stock is only 2% above its annual floor — it is sitting right on the mat. A value of 14% means it has drifted slightly higher but is still near the bottom of its annual range. The lower this number, the more "on sale" the stock currently is relative to its recent history. We cap at 15% to keep only the most beaten-down candidates.
  • ROE — Return on Equity (>10%): A falling stock price does not always mean a failing business. ROE tells you whether the underlying company is still efficiently converting shareholder capital into profit. We require >10% to confirm the business has not deteriorated — we want a temporarily unloved stock, not a structurally broken one. This is the core "quality" gate. A company with ROE <10% is likely falling for a fundamental reason, not a temporary one.
  • Debt/Equity — Debt to Equity Ratio (<1.5x): When a stock is falling, you want to be confident the company can survive the downturn without being crushed by debt obligations. A ratio below 1.5x means the company's debt is manageable relative to its own equity base. High-debt companies near 52-week lows are especially dangerous — a revenue shortfall can trigger a debt spiral. This filter screens them out.
  • Profit Margin (>5%): The stock may be out of favour, but is the company still actually making money? A margin above 5% confirms the business is generating real profit — it is not a zombie company kept alive by accounting adjustments. Thin or negative margins near a 52-week low are a classic "falling knife" profile. We require >5% to filter these out.
  • Trailing P/E (<25): Even near a 52-week low, some stocks are still overvalued relative to their earnings. A P/E below 25 ensures we are buying at a price that reflects the earnings reality, not lingering optimism from a prior bull run. A quality company at a 52-week low with a P/E under 25 is one of the most reliable long-term entry setups in fundamental investing.
  • Quant Score (≥50): Our composite score blends fundamentals, momentum, and mean-reversion signals into a single 0–100 grade. A score of 50 is the minimum "passing grade" — the system has not identified any serious structural red flags across the full picture. This acts as a final cross-check: a stock can pass all four fundamental gates above yet still fail the quant score if there is hidden weakness in its technicals or data quality.

A macro view of overall market health aggregated by sector, sorted by average relative strength.

How to use: Think of this as a "weather report" for the market. Instead of looking at one stock, we look at groups like Tech or Energy.
When to use: Check this first before buying any stock to see if its "neighborhood" is doing well.
The Benefit: It helps you "swim with the tide." It is much easier to make money picking a stock in a sector where the Average RSI is rising and most stocks are above their 50-day average.

Mean Reversion Screener↑ top

Find stocks fundamentally in a long-term uptrend (>200D SMA) experiencing a severe short-term panic sell-off.

How to use: This is a "bargain hunter" tool. It finds stocks that are strong long-term (above their 200-day average) but have suddenly "snapped" like a rubber band due to short-term panic.
When to use: Use this when you want to buy a high-quality stock "on sale" during a temporary dip. The Benefit: It prevents you from "chasing" high prices. Instead, it mathematically identifies a low-risk entry point where the price is likely to "snap back" to its normal level.

Ticker Company Name Sector Country Close Price RSI (14) 200D SMA Dist from 200D (%)
Key Metrics Explained:
  • 200D SMA: The ultimate baseline for long-term institutional health. We only screen for stocks above this line to ensure we aren't catching a "falling knife" in a dying company.
  • Dist from 200D (%): Shows how far the stock has pulled back toward its long-term baseline. A smaller positive number means it is closer to major institutional support.
  • Maximum RSI Threshold: The RSI oscillates on a scale from 0 to 100. A value of 50 is perfectly neutral. A value of 30 or below is mathematically "oversold." This means the asset has been dumped aggressively by sellers without a single break. By setting this threshold to 30, the screener strictly isolates stocks experiencing extreme, irrational short-term fear, which often precedes a sharp "snap back" (bounce).
