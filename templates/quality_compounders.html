Key Metrics Explained:
  • ROE — Return on Equity (>15%): Think of ROE as a measure of how good the company is at turning shareholder money into profit. If you gave a business £100 to invest and it returned £20 profit, that is an ROE of 20% — excellent. A business returning only £5 on every £100 (5% ROE) is inefficient with your capital. We require >15% to ensure management is genuinely skilled at allocating resources, not just surviving on past glories.
  • Margin — Profit Margin (>10%): For every £1 of revenue the company earns, how many pence does it actually keep as profit after paying all its costs? A 10% margin means it keeps 10p. A 2% margin means it keeps 2p — one bad quarter and that margin disappears entirely. High margins signal a genuine competitive advantage (a "moat"): the company can charge premium prices because customers have few alternatives. Businesses with thin margins are fragile — any cost spike (wages, energy, raw materials) can wipe out profitability overnight.
  • Debt/Equity — Debt to Equity Ratio (<1.0): This tells you how much of the business is funded by borrowed money versus the owners' own money. A ratio of 0.5 means for every £1 of equity, the company has borrowed 50p — conservative and safe. A ratio of 3.0 means the company has borrowed £3 for every £1 it actually owns — dangerously exposed if interest rates rise or revenues dip. We cap at <1.0 because heavily indebted companies can look profitable in good times but collapse when conditions get tough.
  • P/E — Price-to-Earnings Ratio (10–35): The P/E ratio tells you how many years of current earnings you are paying for upfront. A P/E of 20 means you are paying £20 today for every £1 of annual profit the company generates.
    Why does the floor matter (P/E < 10)? Extremely cheap stocks are often cheap for a very good reason — the market expects earnings to collapse. These are called "value traps."
    Why does the ceiling matter (P/E > 35)? Very expensive stocks are priced for perfection. Any earnings miss, any slowdown, and the share price falls sharply.
    The 10–35 window targets the sweet spot: solid businesses at fair, sustainable valuations.
  • Quant Score (≥60): Our composite score blends all the above metrics — plus momentum and mean-reversion signals — into a single 0–100 number. Think of it as an overall health grade. A score of 60 is a solid B grade: not perfect, but fundamentally sound across every dimension we measure. Stocks below 60 may pass one or two individual screens but show weakness somewhere else in the picture.
